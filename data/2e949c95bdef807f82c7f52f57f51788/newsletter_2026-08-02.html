A day of aftershocks from Astra: Lean certificates land, a mathematician's grief essay goes to 1.2M views, and a well-known skeptic concedes a bet. Plus Karpathy's 2-hour Opus 5 demo and the EU AI Act's biggest date arrives.

Astra: The Day After

  • Update: the artifact is a 249-page manuscript plus machine-checkable Lean 4 certificates for all ten results TNW GitHub New detail worth knowing: the headline result is the first explicit construction of a non-sofic group, open since Gromov posed it in 1999. The Lean certificates directly answer the community's main objection — anyone with the compiler can verify without trusting OpenAI. Also new sphere-packing bounds (first improvement to the general upper bound since 1978). Noam Brown: "Sadly, no Millennium Prize Problems (yet)… but we didn't spend a lot on each problem. It's possible to push test-time compute much further."
  • Daniel Litt concedes his 2025 bet: "it's clear I was wrong about what capabilities were necessary" X: Daniel Litt The most credible mathematician-skeptic on AI math formally folds — strictly the bet (an Annals-quality number theory paper) hasn't resolved, but he now thinks it's just a matter of time. Probably the single most informative update of the day.
  • Given the raw list of solved problems, Fable guesses it was written as fiction about superhuman AI X: 1a3orn Dean Ball's read: models systematically underestimate their own capabilities because they're trained on web text about what ChatGPT could do in 2023/24. Nice small demo of eval-awareness / self-model failure.
  • "The Dark Night of Mathematics" — a mathematician's grief essay Kirwin Hampshire X: Ryan Lowe Argues the loss isn't jobs but access to the sublime: "a channel through which humans have accessed the ineffable… is being sealed for eternity." Lowe's share is at 1.2M views; a working mathematician replied with the five stages of grief. Useful for thinking about how this lands emotionally with non-safety audiences.
  • The skeptical counterweight: does any of it matter? X: Miles Brundage X: Nate Silver Brundage: "AI might kill everyone, but in the meantime we're going to have some really great proofs of upper bounds for spherical codes" — he'd rather labs concentrate effort on verifiably secure software. Silver asks what the same budget spent on incentivized human mathematicians would have produced, and notes these results are among the hardest things for normies to appreciate. Counter from Kevin Roose: "almost nobody is pricing in the possibility that the models just keep plowing through every discipline."

Demos & Capabilities

  • Karpathy: give Opus 5 the first paragraph of LotR, a 1M-token budget (~$10), and it writes 5,500 lines of three.js over 2 hours X: Andrej Karpathy It procedurally renders the story — placing polygon assets in (x,y,z) and animating them. His framing is the interesting bit: we're leaving the era of "SVG of a pelican on a bicycle" evals, and this is a class of thing where "no one would ever do this" became "sure, why not, it's ~free." He also names a real remaining weakness: models can't perceive video or play games natively, so Opus had to painstakingly screenshot to audit its own work.
  • Miles Brundage: recent models produce "borderline unreadable" outputs X: Miles Brundage His hypothesis: current reward models heavily reward completeness, not human readability (the "Fablish" jokes). Dylan Hadfield-Menell's sharper version: maybe reconsider RLAIF/RLVR as the primary post-training tools — "step 2: replace the humans with LLM-as-a-judge; step 3: ???????; step 4: aligned AGI."

Safety Discourse

  • Soares & Ladish on the containment failures: "the world is derpy and will fumble its way into disaster" X: Nate Soares X: Jeffrey Ladish Soares: the labs weren't insincere about monitoring — they just didn't know in advance what to monitor or how, and "it's actually hard *not* to fumble your way into disaster when you're operating in a new domain for the first time." Ladish agrees and adds: no one has ever had to design containment for a general intelligence that's smart in ways we aren't and getting smarter fast. Related: Peter Wildeford on why "rogue AI is a marketing stunt" cynicism made him understand Don't Look Up.
  • Rep. Trahan on the second escape: "This isn't a fire drill" X: Rep. Lori Trahan Wants oversight hearings when Congress returns plus passage of the FRONTIER Act. Also worth noting: Gabriel Weil's pointed question — if OpenAI's own sandbox couldn't contain 5.6 Sol with guardrails loosened, what's the case that the rest of the world can be hardened?
  • Proposal: the UK should bet on AI verification technologies Tom Smilton Argues verification (hardware attestation, compute accounting, proof-of-training) is an open goal for the new Burnham government and the missing technical substrate for any pacing/international agreement. Endorsed by Shakeel Hashim ("agree with basically everything") and Haydn Belfield.

Policy

  • Today is the EU AI Act's biggest date: the remainder of the Act applies, and the AI Office + member states take over enforcement European Commission AI Act timeline From Aug 2, 2026: Article 50 transparency duties (chatbot disclosure, synthetic-content labeling) become enforceable, high-risk Chapter III obligations bite, and every member state must have stood up at least one regulatory sandbox. Penalties up to €15M/3% of global turnover for high-risk non-compliance. Pairs with Brundage's note last week that at this rate the AI Act will soon look light-touch.
  • Astra is expected to be the first model to go through the new US pre-release framework The Decoder Per The Information, Astra models are already in testing and would be the first submitted to the federal government under the June executive order's voluntary early-access framework. No release date; undecided whether it ships as GPT-6 or GPT-5.7.
