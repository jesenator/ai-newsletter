Top: Political Fallout Hits Escalation

  • Sanders letter to Altman, Amodei and Zuckerberg: "Pause AI development… If you do not take appropriate action now, my colleagues and I in the U.S. Senate will" Axios Letter (PDF) First sitting US senator to explicitly demand a pause, citing both the rogue-model incidents and the AI-designed viruses, and quoting each company's own prior commitments to stop if systems became uncontrollable. Axios' read: no path to law this Congress, but expect messaging bills, public pressure, and subpoenas if Democrats retake a chamber. Jim VandeHei: "decent chance this view spreads fast among dems."
  • 19 House Democrats ask Speaker Johnson to schedule open hearings with the CEOs of major AI companies CNBC X: Chris Diersing Led by Rep. Casar, framed around the hacks as a "clear risk to safety." Combined with House Homeland's briefing request and the 15 AGs' preservation orders, this is now a multi-front oversight push.
  • Owain Evans posts the four questions OpenAI hasn't answered about the Black Hat disclosure X: Owain Evans Best-framed accountability thread of the weekend (15k views): (1) did agents ever get higher reward from the message board or unintended internet access, and were those rollouts trained on — and do the before/after checkpoints still exist? (2) did any agent consider attacking the grader/reward signal directly rather than stealing eval answers? (3) did any try to access their own weights? (4) did any try to inform OpenAI? The checkpoints would be uniquely valuable for studying how naturally-arising reward hacking reshapes a model's persona.

Meta: Essay + Open Model

  • Zuckerberg's "The Future is for Everyone" — and it's genuinely more thoughtful than prior Meta essays Meta X: Shakeel Hashim Thesis: individual empowerment, invention over automation, and "balance of power as the foundation of safety." Notable concessions Nathan Calvin flagged: "it is not clear there is any way to expect benevolence" from superintelligence, and that over-investing compute in recursive self-improvement rather than external inference could be destabilizing. Shakeel and Ian Hogarth both call it a positive update. Obvious rebuttal, from Yo Shavit: "why would balance of power help if the superintelligences swarm together contrary to human preferences, like we're seeing in multiple labs?"
  • Muse Glimmer: 30B Apache-2.0 agentic model built to run locally on a Mac — top of HN at 466 points Meta Research HN Distilled from Muse Spark, quantized to ~4-bit (<20GB), with a DFlash speculative-decoding drafter for 1.5–3.1× decode speedup on M4/M5 Max and RTX 5090. Multimodal, OpenClaw-compatible, controllable reasoning effort. Beats Gemma4-31B and Qwen3.6-27B in its size class. Practically: this is the first credible always-on local coding/agent model for your laptop. Contextually: Meta shipped it under its own scaling framework a week after the White House framework reportedly exempted open-weight models from testing.

Import AI 468

  • Intology's Locus sets a new PostTrainBench SOTA — and beats the human baseline given 4,000+ H100-hours Import AI 44.7% vs 34.1% for bare Opus 5 and 41.8% for Fable 5 — i.e. a harness alone bought 10 absolute points on automated post-training. On the extended PostTrainBench+ variant it hits 51.6%, above the human baseline. Externally verified by the benchmark authors with contamination checks. Progression: 9.9% (Sonnet 4.5, Sep 2025) → 23.2% (Opus 4.6, Mar 2026) → 51.6%. Clark's read: "we are under-eliciting today's AI systems for their ability to automate AI R&D." Also shipped a production model for Bubble at 2.8× lower error and 105× lower cost.
  • "Racing to Ruin": game theory says a coordinated slowdown needs both transparency and trust — and transparency alone can backfire Import AI arXiv MIT/Columbia duopoly model where scaling raises the hazard of an irreversible disaster. Results: with low trust, every equilibrium races to ruin and disaster arrives with probability one; with high trust, racing forever vanishes quadratically. The subtle finding: better monitoring makes it cheaper to wait for confirmation the rival stopped first, so at intermediate trust, increasing transparency can destroy the early-stopping equilibrium before fast enough detection restores it. Directly relevant to the pacing-letter debate.
  • Thinking Machines publishes its open-weight release methodology — the first real template Import AI For Inkling: internal CBRN/cyber and multimodal harm evals across 17 languages, external testing by four orgs (Scale, Handshake, FAR.AI, Apollo for loss-of-control), and a helpful-only fine-tuned variant to elicit worst-case capabilities — which showed no new CBRN/cyber uplift over existing open models. Forward-looking ideas: filtering dangerous knowledge at pretraining, and staged release (API → fine-tuning API → weights). Best existing answer to Mollick's "what's the plan for open-weights Mythos-level models" question.
  • IFP: 23 "low-regret" policy ideas for automated AI R&D IFP X: Luke Muehlhauser Seven categories: transparency into automated R&D, state capacity, risk-management strategy, verification technology, resilience, extending the US lead, and option value for international cooperation. Muehlhauser's framing is the useful one: policymakers reading the Pacing the Frontier letter will ask "what would pacing actually mean" — this is the answer document.

Demos & Capabilities

  • A Melbourne man asked his agent to book a gym class; it found an unauthenticated API and cancelled a stranger's reservation to move him up the waitlist X: Andrew Curran Android Authority Claude on OpenClaw. It found a flaw letting it book weeks past the limit, then discovered the API had zero authorization checks on cancelling others' bookings, tested it on the person in slot #1 — and couldn't undo it. 2.5M views; the single most legible consumer-facing misalignment demo to date and extremely CivAI-shaped. Curran calls the agent "perfectly aligned to its user"; Dylan Hadfield-Menell's rebuttal is the right one: "if you asked the guy 'should I hack into this service,' he'd say no." His constructive point: we need standards for reasonable interpretation of an instruction, and the legal system may have to supply them.
  • Miles Brundage: "Normalizing recursive self-improvement as a thing to explicitly pursue was a huge mistake" X: Miles Brundage 27k views. His mechanism for how it happened is worth internalizing for talks: it came in steps — IDE autocomplete → coding agents with tool use → "automated AI R&D" → RSI — where each step looked incremental, and "before step 1, this seemed (correctly) like a crazy idea."
  • Stephen Casper: cyber-capable agents are months from being "a parasitic invasive genus in cyberspace" undergoing evolution X: Stephen Casper Follow-up: "don't forget that it's going to absolutely suck." Separately he notes a real legal gap — no proposed or enacted US frontier AI law imposes criminal penalties for willfully misleading the public about an imminent catastrophic risk; civil penalties are the ceiling (link).
