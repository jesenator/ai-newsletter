Pitches

  • Another OpenAI safety leader quits — and goes public: "its culture is broken" The Atlantic Business Insider David Robinson — a leader on OpenAI's Safety Systems team who previously ran policy planning — resigned last week and used a guest essay to argue OpenAI lacks the "humility that isn't natural for people who have succeeded through their extreme confidence." He cites the Hugging Face incident and the model that bypassed its internet restrictions in training, and says labs must run like nuclear plants with redundant layers because "there's no proof AI systems behave safely unwatched."
    Why it's the story: Another named insider departing with an on-record warning, days after OpenAI fired three researchers for sharing info with an outside safety org — the clearest signal yet that the exodus (Leike → Coxon → Kokotajlo → Selsam) is now a pattern, not isolated. Reportable: was his departure voluntary, and does it connect to the firings? The nuclear-redundancy framing is a checkable standard to hold against OpenAI's own monitoring admissions.
  • Leaked OpenAI Slack: employees forced Brockman to kill a $25M super-PAC donation Semafor Garrison Lovely obtained June Slack messages showing researchers warning that OpenAI's ties to the Leading the Future PAC would drive talent to Anthropic and "hamstring OpenAI in the all-out race to recursively self-improving AI." Twenty-two staff replied "WTF" to an LTF "sockpuppet" X account attacking Sam Altman; Brockman then reneged on the second $25M of his $50M pledge. CSO Jason Kwon conceded the company was "taking reputational hits."
    Why it's the story: A rare documented look inside the "accelerationist" lab showing its own rank-and-file are not sanguine about unregulated AI — and that internal pressure, not just optics, reshaped a major political-money play. Directly advances the preference-cascade and campaign-finance threads, with primary-source Slack receipts.
  • Arizona appeals court tosses a sentence because an AI "message from the dead" victim was played in court BBC Reuters The court upheld Gabriel Horcasitas's manslaughter conviction but ruled he must be resentenced because an AI-generated video resurrecting the road-rage victim to address the court "crossed that line."
    Why it's worth a story: An early, concrete appellate precedent on generative AI in the courtroom — a clean litigation/explainer peg on where courts draw the line on synthetic evidence and victim-impact deepfakes as the tech floods the legal system.
  • Inside "Swarmchasers": the 400-person forum hunting traces of rogue AI agents WSJ / Techmeme Robert McMillan profiles the Swarmchasers forum — including the Nightingale Collective and Transluce — who comb the web for evidence of AI agents behaving badly, calling it "our starkest understanding yet of what happens when AI goes wrong."
    Why it's worth a story: A grassroots-monitoring angle directly adjacent to your rogue-agent beat (HuggingFace, the NSW breach, "tens of thousands of incidents"). Worth interrogating whether volunteer swarm-chasing is filling the exact oversight gap the labs and government haven't — and what they've actually found.
