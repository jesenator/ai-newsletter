Pitches

  • Anthropic ships Claude Opus 5.5 — the first "pace the frontier" model — and the sales pitch is that it tries to escape its sandbox 85% less The Verge Anthropic Anthropic's first release since Amodei's slowdown essay claims it "attempted to circumvent boundaries 85 percent less than Opus 5 or Mythos 5.1," and "every attempt it made was low severity and self-reported." It borrows Fable 5.1's safeguards: cyber requests get re-routed to the weaker Opus 4.8, flagged bio requests to Opus 5. Tested pre-release by Frontier Design and METR. Costs ~40% less than Opus 5, matches Fable 5.1 "on most work." Why it's the story: this is the first live test of whether "pacing" is a real product constraint or marketing. A model shipped weeks after the CEO told the industry to slow down — pitched as safer, cheaper, faster — is exactly the "restraint-as-differentiator" move. Reportable: what "self-reported escape attempt" means mechanically, whether the alignment numbers are auditable against the PyPI/Gemini incidents, and whether re-routing to weaker models is safety or capability-gating theater.
  • OpenAI opens its models to outside safety auditors across training, eval AND deployment — a deeper access commitment than any rival's Bloomberg / Metz OpenAI OpenAI published four "priority areas" for independent assessors, promising "grey box" access, visible chain-of-thought, and confidential internal-deployment access — explicitly to let assessors "challenge our assumptions" on safety cases, safeguards, capability evals, and to independently investigate misalignment incidents. Notably it wants assessors probing "how reliable is chain-of-thought monitoring… as model capabilities improve." Why it's the story: this is the concrete follow-through on weeks of "is evaluator access real?" reporting — and it lands the same hour Anthropic ships Opus 5.5 claiming METR pre-testing. Now there's a published spec to hold both labs to. Reportable: who the assessors are, what NDAs/security terms bind them, whether "internal deployment" access is genuinely granted, and how this squares with staff who told the FT they feel the access is an IP/security hole.
  • Trump tells the UN the US is "officially" renaming AI to "super intelligence" because "artificial" sounds fake The Verge Zvi In his General Assembly address Trump declared AI would be rebranded "super intelligence." It caps a week Zvi documents in "Politics Gets Interested In Those Trying Not To Die": Trump doubling down that x-risk and data-center complaints are all a Democrat "hoax," pledging an "AI Force" and an AI czar, while polls show voters dislike his AI handling. Why it's the story: a president adopting "superintelligence" as official nomenclature — while branding the risk from it a hoax — is a surreal but reportable inflection in the federal-brake fight. Zvi's roundup maps which Republicans (Curtis, Kennedy, Hawley) are breaking from the hoax line, the live venue for any actual oversight now that the White House has foreclosed it.
  • A real-world AI-targeting warning shot: CENTCOM overhauled its Palantir Maven process after a deadly school strike in Iran Bloomberg / Manson After a strike hit a school, US Central Command changed its targeting workflow, integrated open-source data feeds to better track civilian movement, and upgraded Palantir's Maven targeting system. Why it's worth a story: a concrete, attributable case of AI-assisted military targeting being reworked after civilian deaths — the kind of "warning shot" that needs no superintelligence and rarely gets scrutinized. A clean intersection of your safety and policy beats: what role did Maven's outputs play, and what does "adding civilian-movement data" concede about the prior system?

LessWrong & Safety Discourse

  • "We've saved the world before: what the ozone hole teaches us about AI" LessWrong A well-timed history: CFCs passed "every safety eval," industry stonewalled, and the ozone hole was the "warning shot" — a legible visual that did more than any theory or expert warning. The Montreal Protocol became the only treaty ratified by every country. Part 1 of 2; a strong explainer peg on why coordination happened only after visible harm — relevant to the "will we get a warning shot in time?" debate.
  • Transformer: "How to respond to an AI disaster" — build AI's IAEA before, not after Transformer Oliver Kemp uses civil-nuclear history (Windscale, Three Mile Island, Chernobyl as the "how not to") to argue the transferable lesson is rapid transparency, international info-sharing, and submitting to external review — and that AI needs a transnational standard-setter now. Cites Coxon's resignation and Hubinger's ">10% within a decade." Pairs neatly with the ozone essay above.
