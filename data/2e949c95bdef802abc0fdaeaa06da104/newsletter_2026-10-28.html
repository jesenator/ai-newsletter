  • OpenAI and Anthropic claim their models refuted the Exponential Time Hypothesis — on the same day The Information TechCrunch TNW Both labs say their models produced a deterministic algorithm solving 3-SAT in sub-exponential time, refuting a 27-year-old complexity assumption; OpenAI says it verified the proof in Lean. No practical break of RSA/Bitcoin was demonstrated — but ETH underpins hardness assumptions across cryptography.
    Why it's the story: Two rivals independently announcing the same foundational CS result the same day is itself extraordinary, and the cryptographic framing invites hype inflation. The reportable core is verification: which claim is actually peer-reviewed, whether the Lean formalization is complete, and how much "refuting ETH" really threatens any deployed cryptosystem. A clean explainer-plus-scrutiny piece on AI mathematical claims that the mainstream is covering credulously.
  • A mathematician says OpenAI's Navier–Stokes "solution" may have been seeded by rivals' drafts stored in Codex BBC Hours before OpenAI announced it had "settled" a Navier–Stokes problem in 88 hours, NYU's Tristan Buckmaster and Anthropic-employed mathematician Levent Alpöge went public: they'd been chasing the same result, kept drafts in OpenAI's Codex, and were tipped that their progress had "reached" OpenAI. Buckmaster stopped short of an accusation but asked whether their stored work fed OpenAI's.
    Why it's the story: This is a potential wrongdoing/data-provenance angle hiding inside a feel-good AI-science narrative — did a lab's coding tool ingest independent researchers' unpublished work to score a priority claim? It ties directly to the ETH item above (AI science claims outrunning verification) and to training-data/consent litigation you track. Press OpenAI on Codex data isolation and what "reached us" means.

LessWrong & Safety Discourse

  • Forethought: "Hard cases for AI behavioural design" — when legal orders produce coups Forethought A new paper lays out scenarios where AI's behavioral desiderata collide: a general orders an AI command-and-control layer of autonomous units to seize the Capitol, backed by a corrupted but "verified" legal memo; or a lawful order to consolidate the entire automated military into one terminal. Should the AI defer to human legal authority or override it? The authors offer no solutions — the point is that existing law wasn't written for AI this capable.
    Why it matters: A rigorous, concrete framing of the "follow the law vs. prevent catastrophe" tradeoff that's otherwise abstract — directly relevant as DeepMind staff resign over Pentagon deals and the Army fields autonomous cyber agents. Great raw material for an explainer on why "just make AI obey the law" is harder than it sounds.
