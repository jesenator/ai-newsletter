Pitches

  • The "warning shot" is no longer hypothetical: AI is now scaling live attacks on US water and power infrastructure AI Safety Newsletter CAIS pulls together a striking picture: CISA warned Aug 19 that AI-generated code is being used against water, energy and chemical plants; Iranian hackers allegedly shut a UK power plant for four days in July; and 100+ US water facilities (30 in Minnesota) were hit this summer. FAI's analysis explains the mechanism—AI makes it economical to run massive parallel attacks on decentralized, low-value targets that were never worth a human's time. Separately, Bloomberg reports Chinese state hackers using DeepSeek (chosen for weak guardrails + low cost) more than doubled their attack volume.
    Why it's the story: This is the cleanest real-world instantiation of the offense-favoring cyber-uplift thesis the safety community has warned about—decentralized critical infrastructure as the soft target, and a behind-the-frontier open model (DeepSeek) doing the heavy lifting. It directly arms the open-weights-governance fight and Trump's new Project Watershed 250 pilot in Texas. Reportable: attribution on the 100+ water hits, and whether DeepSeek's guardrails are the policy crux.
  • AI is a "worryingly-good persuader"—but the bottleneck is attention, not persuasiveness Transformer Oxford's Oliver Kemp walks through the evidence: the UK AISI study (42,000 participants, 19 models) found chatbots shifted political views ~10 points and beat static messaging by 41–52%. Post-training for persuasion mattered more than model size; "information density" was the winning tactic—but the most persuasive setting also produced false claims in nearly a third of responses. A Nature study found AI conversations beat video ads at changing candidate preferences; one estimate puts LLM persuasion at $48–75 per persuaded voter vs. $100 traditional.
    Why it's the story: A rigorous explainer that both substantiates and deflates the "uber-persuasion" panic—the real risk isn't forced robocalls (people resist overt persuasion) but the billion daily voluntary chats where "exposure is solved by the user." That reframes the persuasion-safety debate toward baked-in model skew rather than manipulation, and it's a rare piece with named AISI authors (Ben Tappin) on record hedging their own findings.
  • SB Energy's IPO filing says it is "substantially dependent" on OpenAI—the circular-financing risk made explicit in an S-1 CNBC / Techmeme The SoftBank/OpenAI/Nvidia-backed AI power-infrastructure company filed to go public and flagged its dependence on OpenAI as a material risk.
    Why it's the story: This is the off-balance-sheet, cross-investment web I've tracked all month surfacing in a regulated disclosure document—the same "disorderly correction" scenario BoE's Bailey warned about yesterday, now legible in an SEC filing. An IPO whose fortunes hinge on a single unprofitable customer (OpenAI) is a testable data point for the AI-capex-bubble thesis. Reportable: how much of SB Energy's contracted capacity is OpenAI, and what happens to bondholders if OpenAI slows its buildout.
  • ICE wants to spend up to $2M on Boston Dynamics robot dogs for immigration enforcement WBUR A funding document shows ICE plans to buy quadruped robots from the Massachusetts maker—whose usage policy has historically barred weaponization but is murkier on surveillance/enforcement deployment.
    Why it's the story: The robotics + civil-liberties + usage-policy collision in one procurement, landing amid the Flock/Palantir surveillance backlash I've tracked. It tests whether Boston Dynamics' acceptable-use terms constrain a federal enforcement buyer the way Anthropic's did with the Pentagon (per Judge Lin's ruling). Reportable: what tasks ICE specifies, whether Boston Dynamics can or will object, and how this squares with its no-weaponization pledge.
