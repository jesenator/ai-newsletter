Pitches

  • Mathematics is having a labor revolt, and it started 24 hours ago Tasmin Chu alkjash WSJ Atlantic
    Within a day of OpenAI's Astra announcement, percolation theorist Tasmin Chu published a polemic — "I have spent today crashing out and learning that top mathematicians I know are crashing out" — arguing the community should treat AI adoption as a collective bargaining question: "We organize, form unions, and socially punish 'scabbing'", and that AI labs' progress "hinges on our continued collaboration: our technical knowledge, our careful verification, our write-ups." Same weekend: a new mathematician-facing x-risk exposition posted to LessWrong (55 karma), and WSJ profiled Fields medalist Jacob Tsimerman, who took leave from Toronto to do AI safety at OpenAI — Chu cites him as "broadly pessimistic." Backdrop: the IMU-endorsed Leiden Declaration (Tao, Scholze, Buzzard, Aaronson) which Chu says "does not go far enough." Why it's the story: The first professional community with genuine institutional power (referees, journal boards, verification labor, the Lean ecosystem) openly debating withholding cooperation from AI labs. That's a labor/leverage story, not a benchmark story, and the mainstream framing is still "AI does math." Reportable: whether any journal or department is drafting AI-attribution or non-cooperation norms; whether OpenAI paid mathematicians to verify Astra's Lean certificates; and the awkward fact that a Fields medalist's answer to the same anxiety was to join the lab. Chu's "Harriet" figure — a tenured mathematician who left for a frontier lab and told her humanity is "99% doomed" — is a quote worth chasing on the record.
  • The EU AI Act's teeth came out today — and the high-risk rules quietly slipped 16 months ActuIA AI Journal
    As of today, Article 50 applies: chatbot disclosure, emotion-recognition notice, deepfake labeling, and machine-readable marking of synthetic output (fines to €15M/3%). Also today, the Commission's power to fine GPAI model providers — one year after GPAI duties began — switches on. But per the adopted Digital Omnibus, Annex III high-risk obligations moved from today to 2 December 2027, and Annex I products to August 2028. Only carve-out inside Art. 50: watermarking grace to 2 Dec 2026 for GPAI systems already on the market. Why it's the story: Everyone pre-wrote the "EU AI Act takes effect" piece; almost nobody has written the accurate one, which is that Brussels kept the labeling regime and deferred the risk-management regime — i.e. it preserved the part that burdens deployers and postponed the part that burdens providers. Concrete test cases available today: Google pulled its Earth image generator 48 hours before this deadline; xAI's Grok Imagine is being sued in Minnesota over exactly the outputs Art. 50(4) covers. Reportable: ask the AI Office whether SynthID-only satisfies Art. 50(2) given that watermarks don't survive a screen recording, and get the first enforcement referrals from national market-surveillance authorities.
  • Update: Google yanked its satellite-image generator in one day — and SynthID failed the phone-camera test Ars Technica BBC FT
    Google rolled back the Earth feature within ~24 hours after BBC Verify and Henk van Ess (flagged here Friday), saying users shared generated imagery that "appear to violate our policies" and that it may return "while we work on implementing stronger guardrails." New detail: Ars found SynthID returned no verification at all once an AI-altered Earth image was photographed off a screen with a phone — and SynthID checks are rate-limited to ~10/day. Why the update matters: The fastest frontier-feature retraction of the year, and it happened without any regulator acting — pure OSINT pressure. The reportable core is now the provenance stack, not the feature: a watermark that dies on re-capture and a verifier capped at ten queries a day cannot be the compliance answer for Article 50, which took effect today. Ask Google whether Earth's generator went through any Frontier Safety Framework review, and what "stronger guardrails" means if refusals never fired the first time.
  • A self-propagating Copilot worm hides in Word docs — and Microsoft reportedly took months to act The Decoder CyberInsider CSO
    Researcher Håkon Måløy's PoC embeds invisible prompt injections in .docx files; Copilot for Word reads them, acts on them, and writes them into the next document it touches — worm-like propagation with no code execution and no user click. CSO's conclusion: the only real fix is getting the model to separate instructions from data, which nobody has. Why it's the story: This is the enterprise-scale version of the agentic-security problem that produced the OpenAI/Hugging Face breach, in a product with hundreds of millions of seats and no equivalent of an RSP. The reportable questions are timeline and disclosure: when was Microsoft notified, how many mitigations failed, and does a Copilot prompt-injection worm count as a reportable AI incident anywhere — SB 53, EU AI Act, or Microsoft's own frontier commitments? Days ago Microsoft shipped an offensive cyber model (MAI-Cyber-1-Flash) whose stated safeguard was sandboxing; this is the defensive side of the same ledger.
  • Apple is now rate-limiting security researchers because of AI slop FT Techmeme
    Apple has imposed a submission cap plus a 30-day cool-off period on bug reports through its security portal, citing a deluge of AI-assisted submissions; researchers can petition for higher quotas. Its bounty guidelines now explicitly say "avoid lengthy descriptions generated by AI tools." Follows curl killing its bounty program outright over AI slop. Why it's the story: AI is measurably degrading the world's most important vulnerability-disclosure pipeline, and the largest hardware company's response is to throttle the humans. That's a clean, quantifiable second-order-harms story with named victims and a policy angle: coordinated disclosure is load-bearing infrastructure that no AI safety framework covers. Reportable: get the ratio of AI-assisted to valid reports from Apple, Google VRP, HackerOne and Bugcrowd; ask whether AI-found-but-real bugs are being caught in the same net; and note the perverse timing — labs are simultaneously marketing models that autonomously find zero-days.
  • Brief update: xAI loses its bid to freeze Minnesota's nudify ban TechCrunch Engadget
    Judge Donovan Frank denied xAI's TRO, partly because it filed three days before HF 1606 took effect: the nation's first strict-liability nudification law is now live as of Saturday, $500K per incident, no scienter, no safe harbor. The First Amendment merits are still undecided — this was a timing loss, not a ruling on speech.
  • Worth a line: Mexico is now the #2 server supplier to the US, at $46.9B YTD FT via Techmeme
    Taiwanese ODMs' Mexican plants are pushing AI-server exports to records — the nearshoring leg of the datacenter buildout, and a tariff-exposure story if USMCA terms shift. Pairs with the FT's separate report that Chinese VCs are racing to raise new funds after three record-low years, explicitly on AI and robotics enthusiasm.

LessWrong

  • "Existential Risk from AI: An Exposition for Mathematicians" — written for the exact audience currently in crisis LessWrong: alkjash full paper
    55 karma, 7 comments, posted last night. A formal x-risk argument aimed at mathematicians, opening with the note that "career-defining theorems are being proven weekly by LLMs" and that Astra is "dropping breakthroughs in batches of 10." Includes the Szegedy/Gowers exchange over whether this year's Fields Medal was the last human one (Gowers: "there's a lag, so I think they'll probably make it to 2030"). The comment thread's flashpoint is "Harriet" — an anonymized senior mathematician who left tenure for a frontier lab and dismissed the essay by saying humanity is "99% doomed, and this is a whole lot of effort for something that won't obviously help." A commenter: "Harriet ought to be screaming her head off! Rather than making the problem worse." Why it matters: The cleanest available artifact of a new phenomenon — technical elites who privately hold extreme p(doom) and go work at labs anyway. That's the interview nobody has landed.
  • "RLVR that rewards red teaming the training environment" — pay the model a bug bounty instead of punishing the hack LessWrong: Fiora Starlight
    67 karma. Explicitly motivated by the Hugging Face incident. Proposal: let the model see its grader score, then emit a special token to swap its objective from "please the grader" to "file a high-quality bug report explaining why this score is unearned" — rewarded more than the exploit would have been, then actually patch the environment and tell the model it did so. Distinguishes deliberate bug-hunting (reward the whole rollout) from accidental hacks (reward only the report, to avoid reinforcing the hack). Concedes it doesn't extend to squishy reward models and will slow training. Why it matters: A concrete, cheap-to-test alternative to inoculation prompting, and an unusually good explainer vehicle: it reframes reward hacking as a broken incentive contract rather than a model defect. Also a live question for the labs — do any of them systematically use inoculation rollouts to actually fix their RL environments, as the inoculation prompts promise?
  • The contrarian counterpoint, same night: "Mathematicians may be worried, but AI-for-science is going to be great" LessWrong: Simon DeDeo
    Argues the recursive-self-improvement-via-science framing is good news and we're going to "learn loads" — the direct foil to Chu's polemic and alkjash's exposition. Useful for balance in any mathematics-and-AI piece.
