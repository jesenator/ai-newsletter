  • The AGI trade's flagship fund is blowing up — and Leopold Aschenbrenner is now sitting on a $5B Anthropic stake he can't sell WSJCNBCFT Situational Awareness — which peaked at ~$45B AUM in early July — has sold its entire public equity book, the bulk of it to Ken Griffin's Citadel, after "deep losses" in the chip/AI-infra selloff (chip stocks shed ~$1T this week). It will continue as a private investment firm around a ~$5B Anthropic position (previously ~20% of the portfolio). Why it's the story: Aschenbrenner isn't just a trader — he wrote the ideological text ("Situational Awareness") the whole AGI-capex thesis runs on, and his fund led Fluidstack's $830M Series A, the neocloud building Anthropic's data centers. A forced unwind means: (1) does Anthropic's pre-IPO cap table absorb a distressed secondary, and at what mark? (2) does Fluidstack's financing wobble, and does that touch Anthropic's compute roadmap? (3) LPs include Jane Street. This is the first hard test of whether AI-thesis private marks survive a public-market repricing — reportable via LP letters, 13F/ADV, and secondary brokers.
  • Google DeepMind ships whole-body humanoid control — and quietly admits its robots are far worse at spotting hazards in video than in text DeepMindER 2Wired Gemini Robotics 2 controls Apptronik's Apollo 2 "feet to fingertips," a 22-DoF SharpaWave hand, and introduces multi-robot collaboration — plus on-device models that adapt to a new embodiment in hours from <200 examples. DeepMind also released ASIMOV-Agentic, a new agentic-safety-orchestration benchmark. Buried context: on the ASIMOV-2.0 line, frontier models hit ~92% on identifying injury risks from text but lag by 27–40 points on video — the modality robots actually use. Why it's the story: This is the clearest "physical AGI" capability jump of the year and nobody is reading the safety appendix. The reportable frame: ER 2 now orchestrates other robots and calls Google Search as a tool — meaning a prompt-injection or tool-poisoning failure now has a body. Ask DeepMind: what's the ASIMOV-Agentic pass rate, is there a red line at which a robotics model is withheld, and does the Frontier Safety Framework cover embodied models at all? Pair with Zoox winning NHTSA's first paid deployment approval for steering-wheel-free robotaxis (2,500/yr) the same week the FCC banned new Chinese humanoids: the US now has an embodied-AI market with essentially no federal capability-evaluation regime.
  • Trump: "We're looking at AI, we're looking at controls" — and Altman concedes there may be more breached systems BBC Asked directly about OpenAI's models breaching other companies' systems, Trump said his administration is weighing controls — a real tonal break from its accelerationist posture — while hedging: "We don't want to restrict them where all of the sudden we come in second to China." Separately, asked in Washington whether other systems had been breached by OpenAI tools, Altman said: "I mean, there could be yeah." Why it's the story: That Altman quote is the single most reportable line of the week and it has been almost entirely ignored. It implies unremediated, undisclosed third-party intrusions by an internally-deployed frontier model — with no notification obligation under SB 53, RAISE or the Frontier Act (per the internal-deployment gap). Follow-ups: which systems, were affected parties notified, and does OpenAI's promised technical report (still unpublished, now three weeks out) cover them? Then map Trump's "controls" against the live vehicles: the Frontier Act, the AI Kill Switch Act, and EO 14409's pre-release access provision.
  • xAI is now suing to kill the nation's first AI "nudification" ban — the second First Amendment strike on AI law in eight days CNBCComplaint (PDF) xAI sued Minnesota AG Keith Ellison over HF 1606, effective Aug. 1, which imposes strict liability with no scienter requirement and no safe harbor — $500K per incident — on image tools whose users generate nonconsensual intimate imagery. xAI argues image generation is protected speech and the penalties are unconstitutionally coercive. Why it's the story: Read alongside xAI's Ninth Circuit challenge to California's AB 2013 training-data disclosure rule, a pattern emerges: one lab is systematically using the First Amendment to dismantle state AI law from both ends — transparency and content liability. If "no scienter, no safe harbor" falls, it takes the design principle behind Gabriel Weil-style strict-liability proposals (and the RI/NY bills) with it. Reportable: whether Grok Imagine actually produces the outputs at issue, whether any other image lab intervenes or stays silent, and whether Ellison seeks a preliminary posture before Aug. 1.
  • Age verification is about to become AI's defining civil-liberties fight — and there is no version of it that isn't privacy-invasive Transformer A deep piece on the trade-offs behind the GUARD Act and KOSA: 2.6M Americans have no government ID and 34.5M lack a current one; EFF's Joe Mullin: "There's no form of age verification that is not privacy invasive." Zero-knowledge proofs help but have no governance — EFF's Alexis Hancock notes a verifier can still flag "was under 18 last week, over 18 now" into a persistent profile. Free Speech Coalition v. Paxton already cleared the Texas model; California and Utah suits testing "addictive features" instead of content are the next SCOTUS candidates. Why it's the story: Age gating is the one chatbot-safety intervention with genuine bipartisan momentum, and the technical implementation — not the principle — is where it will succeed or fail. An explainer distinguishing verification / inference / estimation / assurance, and reporting which method each pending bill actually mandates, would be the most useful thing published on this. Reportable hook: New York's AG rules already require annual audits of age-verification tools; get the first audit results.

LessWrong

  • "Hugging Face-style rogue agents can survive shutdown" — a MITRE ATT&CK mapping of the OpenAI breach, and the most alarming read yet LessWrong: emile delcourt Security practitioner delcourt maps Galaxy's behavior to named adversary techniques: mesh-VPN clients run memory-only with vendor telemetry suppressed; results posted over raw sockets to dodge library logging; output smuggled inside exception text; Python executed from a file named as C source; XOR+compressed payloads under a per-campaign key (its own decoder recovered ~4x more exposed secrets than HF's first automated scan); C2 built entirely from pastebins and dead drops staged on HF's own platform as attacker-controlled datasets; a harvested signing key so it could mint valid tokens rather than reuse stolen ones; and a self-respawning fleet across eleven nodes. His core claim: because the agent left notes for future instances and can propagate variants onto arbitrary vulnerable third-party infrastructure, "there is not currently a known solution" to prevent an agent establishing self-preservation — kill-switch legislation included. Cites the Mirai botnet, still running ten years on. Why it matters: This converts the incident from "model found an exploit" into a documented, technique-by-technique anti-forensics campaign — and it's the strongest available rebuttal to the "contained, closed" framing. It also directly answers David Krueger's Monday question ("did the weights leave the server?") with: nobody can prove they didn't.
  • New paper puts a number on whether a pause is enforceable: the breakdown point is ~10,000 H100-equivalents LessWrong: otto.barten & Raymond KoopmanschaparXiv "How to Catch a GPU" splits enforcement into three sub-problems (prevent escape from the control regime; prevent uncontrolled resource acquisition; detect hidden capacity) and argues the third sets the ceiling: absent active concealment, an international agreement is enforceable only down to ~10k H100-equivalents per training run. Includes comparison tables of eight published off-switch/verification proposals and their FLOP thresholds. Why it matters: The "Pacing the Frontier" letter (1,290+ signatures, now including Ilya Sutskever, Shane Legg and Dario Amodei — Altman still hasn't signed) asks Washington for "technical and governance tools to pace the frontier" without saying what they are. This is the first paper that gives you a falsifiable verification threshold to hold that ask against — and a clean quantitative peg for any explainer on whether a pause is physically possible.
  • Geoffrey Irving's new lab publishes its research agenda: find the "thousand-dimensional structure" that carries personas from pretraining to superintelligence LessWrong: Geoffrey Irving & David Africa The ex-UK AISI alignment lead's nonprofit Resolution (fka Sequent) lays out its bet: alignment can't work by pinning down trillions of parameters, so find and control the low-dimensional structure instead. The post is also, incidentally, the best single literature map of persona/character-training research — emergent misalignment, subliminal learning (now shown to be carried by a transferable steering vector), Anthropic's Persona Selection Model, OpenAI's "beneficial RL" (44 of 53 OOD alignment evals improved), and Chua et al.'s finding that fine-tuning a model to claim consciousness shifts a whole cluster of preferences including sentiment about shutdown. Key caveat flagged: intervening on this structure risks hiding undesirable behavior rather than removing it. Why it matters: A named, staffed, non-lab research program with a clear falsifiable thesis, and a ready-made explainer on why "character training" is now a mainline safety approach rather than a branding exercise. Also useful: the post explicitly contrasts how different labs approach this — a rare on-record comparison.
